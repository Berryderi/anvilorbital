What we've found so far

Desk research and simple models from November 2026. Some of it argues against our own idea, and we'd rather show that than hide it.

How far to trust this: no spacecraft engineer or lawyer has checked it, and every cost is an unchecked assumption. We've had one informal industry call and no customer interviews. We used AI tools for the research and the models. This isn't engineering, legal or investment advice. Tell us if you find a mistake.

  1. 01

    Altitude decides whether retirement is even a problem

    New low-orbit satellites have five years after their mission ends to come down, under US and Canadian rules.

    In our model, large-array satellites at or below about 650 km mostly clear that limit with no help. At 750 km and above, they mostly miss it. Compact satellites miss it at every altitude we ran, at average solar activity.

    Trust the split, not the exact years. A second method gave the same split, and the model runs 1.8 to 4.3 times shorter than one published rough guide.

  2. 02

    A working satellite can retire itself cheaply

    Lowering a 3-tonne satellite from 700 km takes about 46 kg of propellant in our model, or about $159k with launch at $500/kg. That excludes design and operations cost. So burning up is our real competitor, and a paid service matters mostly for satellites that fail before they can lower themselves.

  3. 03

    Parts alone don't pay

    Collecting a dead satellite and taking out its parts costs about $18.6M per satellite in our model, and about $3M with every input in our favour. The parts are worth at most about $750k if launch costs $3,245/kg, and $46k to $116k at the $200 to $500/kg that orbital data center plans need. Cheaper launch makes used parts worth less. We found no buyer for them.

  4. 04

    Data center nodes depend on which satellite you start with

    High-power satellites: ten years of a retiring 3-tonne satellite's power is worth about $0.97M to $5.10M in our model. Servicing it costs about $8.32M at default inputs and $0.80M at best. So this needs servicing about 7 to 10 times cheaper, and a buyer for the compute. We haven't found one.

    Older, smaller satellites: one 200 kW node would take about 82 of them, at about $246M with best inputs. A new unit costs about $2.9M to $11M to launch in the same model.

  5. 05

    The money so far is small, and mostly government

    By our rough count, the world spends about $80M a year on removing and disposing of satellites, nearly all of it government money. That count uses assumed contract lengths. We haven't asked anyone to pay us.

  6. 06

    The hardest hardware step is still unproven

    We found no completed capture of an unprepared dead satellite in low orbit. Katalyst's Link, a $30M NASA-sponsored effort to rescue the Swift telescope, re-entered in September 2026 without reaching it. Astroscale's project manager on its ADRAS-J inspection mission called the work "far more difficult than expected".

  7. 07

    A dead satellite still belongs to someone

    The Outer Space Treaty, Article VIII, says ownership "is not affected" by being in space. So we'd only work with owners who agree. There is a precedent for agreed servicing: Northrop's MEV-1 docked with an Intelsat client satellite in 2020. About half of dead low-orbit satellites are Russian or Soviet, and we treat those as off limits.
